### 1. The Color Palette (The "Golden Hour")
Morning light is defined by its low angle and atmospheric scattering.
*   **Warm Highlights:** The direct light isn't white; it is rich with amber, peach, and pale gold tones. It tends to "warm up" surfaces, making cold concrete look beige and green leaves look yellowish-green.
*   **Cool Shadows:** Unlike the pitch black of night, morning shadows often hold deep blues and purples. This creates a pleasing visual contrast (complementary colors) against the golden highlights.

### 2. The Geometry of Light
*   **Long Shadows:** Because the sun is low on the horizon, every vertical object (trees, buildings, people) casts a long, stretched-out shadow. This exaggerates textures—a bumpy road looks bumpier; a textured wall looks more rugged.
*   **God Rays (Volumetric Light):** If there is any moisture, dust, or fog in the air, the light becomes visible as physical beams cutting through the environment.

### 3. Texture and Atmosphere
*   **Dew and Gloss:** In an outdoor setting, surfaces are often wet with dew. Morning light makes grass, car hoods, and spiderwebs sparkle with high-contrast specular highlights (tiny bright white dots).
*   **Mist and Haze:** Morning often brings a low-hanging mist that softens the background. This increases depth perception—the foreground is sharp and colorful, while the background fades into a pale, foggy silhouette.
