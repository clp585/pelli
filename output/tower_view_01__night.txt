Here are three examples of how night lighting changes a scene:

### 1. If the scene is a **City Street**:
*   **The Palette:** The daylight colors are replaced by high-contrast tones. The sky is a deep, bruised purple or charcoal gray, not pitch black.
*   **Artificial Light:** Streetlamps cast pools of sickly orange or buzzing sodium-yellow light. Neon signs reflect vividly on car hoods and shop windows, creating streaks of red, electric blue, and hot pink.
*   **Shadows:** Shadows are long and sharp. Alleyways and doorways become abysses of total blackness.
*   **Texture:** If it is raining, the pavement looks like black glass, doubling every light source.

### 2. If the scene is a **Forest or Natural Landscape**:
*   **The Palette:** Everything becomes monochromatic cool tones—silvers, steel blues, and deep indigos. Green foliage turns nearly black.
*   **Moonlight:** If the moon is out, it acts as a harsh, pale spotlight (rim-lighting), catching the edges of leaves, branches, or rocks, creating silvery outlines against the dark.
*   **Visibility:** Depth perception drops. The background isn't a horizon anymore; it is a wall of darkness. Fog or mist might glow faintly where the moonlight hits it.

### 3. If the scene is an **Interior (Living Room/Bedroom)**:
*   **The Palette:** The ambient light is gone. The room feels smaller and more intimate.
*   **Practical Lights:** Illumination comes from specific sources—the soft, warm glow of a bedside lamp, the harsh blue flicker of a television screen, or a rectangle of pale street lighting stretching across the floor from the window.
*   **Mood:** Corners of the room disappear into shadow. Dust motes might dance in the single beam of light.
